# Signal Card Examples

Last updated: 2026-04-02

A living library of signal card examples organized by trigger type and call stage. These are reference examples — what a signal card should look and feel like. As beta data comes in, replace synthetic examples with real ones that landed well.

**Format for each entry:**
- **Trigger:** The exact moment/words that caused the card to fire
- **Card text:** What appears on screen (short — fits in ~20 words)
- **Why it works:** The principle behind the card

---

## Objections — Close Stage

### "That's a lot of money"
**Trigger:** Prospect says some version of "that's a lot," "that's more than I expected," or long silence after price reveal.

**Card:** *"Don't defend the price. Ask: 'What's it costing you to stay where you are?'"*

**Why it works:** Defending the price is the instinct. It's also wrong. When you defend, you're arguing. When you ask about cost-of-status-quo, you're returning to pain — which is what got them interested in the first place. The prospect is comparing $X to their bank account. Wingman's job is to redirect the comparison to what staying stuck costs them every month.

---

### "I need to think about it"
**Trigger:** Prospect uses "think about it," "sleep on it," or "give me some time" after price is revealed.

**Card:** *"Don't push. Ask: 'What specifically is making you hesitate?' Then go quiet."*

**Why it works:** "I need to think about it" almost never means they need time. It means they have an unspoken objection. The rep's job is to find it. Pushing them to decide faster creates resistance. Asking what's specifically making them hesitate surfaces the real objection so it can be handled.

---

### "I need to talk to my spouse / partner"
**Trigger:** Prospect raises a third-party decision maker late in the call.

**Card:** *"[Name] — when you say talk to [partner], what do you think they're going to say?"*

**Why it works:** This is a pattern interrupt. Most prospects use "talk to my spouse" as a soft exit. Asking what the spouse will say forces them to articulate the real objection. If the spouse is genuinely the blocker, the rep now knows and can handle it. If it's an excuse, the prospect's answer will reveal that too. Uses prospect's name for tie-down effect.

---

### "I'm not sure if now is the right time"
**Trigger:** Prospect expresses timing hesitation — "maybe in a few months," "let me get through Q1 first," etc.

**Card:** *"What does waiting 90 days cost you? What do you lose if this doesn't change by then?"*

**Why it works:** The Gordon Gap — quantify the distance between where they are and where they want to be, multiplied by time. "What does waiting cost?" is more powerful than "but think about what you'll gain." Loss aversion is stronger than opportunity gain in human psychology.

## Discovery — Excavating Pain

### Prospect volunteers a big problem, rep moves on
**Trigger:** Prospect mentions something painful ("I lose deals every time I have to discuss price") and the rep immediately moves to the next question.

**Card:** *"Go back. '[Prospect's exact words]' — ask them to tell you more about that."*

**Why it works:** Reps trained to cover a list of questions often skip past the biggest pain because they're moving to the next item. The signal card catches this and uses the prospect's exact words to remind the rep what they said, creating a natural pivot: "You mentioned [their words] — can you tell me more about that?"

---

### Rep is moving through Situation too fast
**Trigger:** Rep has spent less than 5 minutes in Discovery and is already transitioning toward pitch.

**Card:** *"Slow down. You haven't found the real pain yet. Ask: 'What have you already tried?'"*

**Why it works:** "What have you already tried?" is the question that surfaces emotional investment and qualifies the problem as real. If they've tried nothing, the pain isn't urgent. If they've tried multiple things, the pain is deep. Either way, the rep needs this answer before pitching. Rushing to the solution before understanding the problem is the most common rookie mistake Wingman is designed to catch.

## Pre-Pitch — Confirm Before You Pitch

### Rep about to pitch without confirming
**Trigger:** Rep uses transition language ("so what I'd love to show you is...," "let me explain how this works") without having completed a Confirm step.

**Card:** *"Confirm first. 'Before I show you what we do — did we cover everything important to you?'"*

**Why it works:** Pitching before confirming is the second most expensive mistake in a sales call (after not finding pain). The prospect is thinking about something you didn't address, and you're presenting a solution to a problem they've already mentally moved past. The Confirm step is 30 seconds and kills ghost objections that would otherwise surface at Close.

## Transition / Bridge

### Rep jumping straight from Discovery to Pitch
**Trigger:** Rep completes Discovery questions and immediately begins presenting the offer without a Bridge.

**Card:** *"Summarize their pain first. 'So from what you've shared, [their pain in their words]... is that right?'"*

**Why it works:** The Bridge does two things: (1) it proves the rep was listening — which builds massive trust, and (2) it anchors the pitch to the specific problem they described, making the solution feel inevitable rather than generic. A pitch without a Bridge feels like a PowerPoint. A pitch after a Bridge feels like a prescription.

## Close — Price Reveal Silence

### After price reveal — rep speaks first
**Trigger:** Rep reveals price and then immediately speaks — doesn't allow silence.

**Card:** *"Stop talking. The next person who speaks loses. Wait."*

**Why it works:** After price reveal, silence is the rep's most powerful weapon. The impulse to fill silence with justification ("but it includes...") signals insecurity and invites negotiation. Staying quiet signals confidence. The prospect will break the silence with either a yes, an objection (which can be handled), or a question (also handleable). None of those outcomes are worse than the rep immediately defending the price unprompted.

## Framework Coverage — Stages Skipped

### Rep never pressed on cost of inaction
**Trigger:** Transcript analysis shows the call went from Pain to Pitch without a Press stage (quantifying the cost of not solving the problem).

**Card:** *"Before you pitch: 'What does this cost you every month you don't fix it?'"*

**Why it works:** The Press stage is the most commonly skipped and the most important for high-ticket closing. Reps who skip Press get to Close and find the prospect isn't emotionally ready to make a decision — because the cost of inaction never felt real to them. This card fires when Wingman detects the transition is happening too early.

## Notes on Card Language

- Cards should sound like the rep's inner voice, not a text book
- Never use jargon the rep wouldn't say themselves ("leverage the NEPQ framework")
- Use the prospect's name when it's available in the transcript
- Use the prospect's exact words in quotation marks whenever possible
- Keep cards under 20 words where possible — the rep is mid-call, not reading a paragraph
- Phrase cards as a directive, not a suggestion ("Ask:" not "You might want to consider asking:")
